A note before you read

Builders and friends keep asking me where to start with AI for their company. This guide is my answer. I run a building company and use these tools in our office every day.

It is based on Claude, which I prefer, in part because Anthropic is focused on business and enterprise customers. ChatGPT is very similar and also a great product. Nearly everything here carries over.

Read it in order the first time. It is current as of October 2026, but AI is changing fast and any of this could update, change or get easier at any point, so check back for updates.

Josh Rosenthal, Lock Ten Studio

What Claude is

Claude is an AI assistant you work with in plain English, by typing or by talking. You can use it in a web browser, in the desktop app on your computer or on your phone. The same Claude handles a quick question and real work on your files.

This guide is about business. The value is in getting work done: creating efficiencies, letting builders build and getting the paperwork out of the way.

Two breakthroughs made that real. First, these tools learned not only to read your files but to produce and change files on your computer, which made AI a work tool instead of an email editor. Second, Claude Code became usable by regular people, which let us build our own tools.

Three ideas carry the rest of this guide:

  • It is only as good as what you give it.
  • The first answer is a draft.
  • You stay responsible for anything that leaves the office.

Start here: the first fifteen minutes

Sign up for Pro at claude.com ($20 a month; Plans and privacy explains why not the free plan). Before you put anything in, go to Settings, then Privacy, and turn off "Help improve Claude."

Then pick one real task from your week. Something real but low stakes:

  • Turn your notes from yesterday's site walk into a punch list grouped by room.
  • Read one subcontractor proposal and list what it includes, what it leaves out and what is vague.
  • Help you answer a homeowner email you have been putting off.

Give it the one file that matters and tell it what you want, the way you would brief a new project coordinator.

The first answer will usually be technically fine. What it won't do yet is sound like you or look like your best work. Tell it so in plain words. "Too formal, our clients are our neighbors." "We always lead with the schedule, then the cost." Then ask again.

That loop (ask, read, correct, ask again) is the most important habit in this guide. It is how you give Claude your style, your taste and your perspective until the work reads like yours. Most people who give up on AI quit after the first answer.

Gain trust as you go

Context is the whole game. On its own Claude is a generic tool. Access to your data and your context is what turns it into something built around your business, and each step below gives it a little more.

Trust runs both ways. It is also about trusting yourself and your own learning. As you get better at this, it becomes safer to hand over more data and try harder things, because you have done it before. Move up when the step you are on has earned it.

Step 1 of 6

Ask a question

Try

Ask how to explain winter gaps in a new hardwood floor to a homeowner, short enough to text.

Good looks like

An answer you would have been comfortable writing. Check anything about local code.

Move up

When you use its answers more than you rewrite them.

Step 2 of 6

Give it one file

Try

Attach a subcontractor proposal and ask what is included, excluded, carried as an allowance or unclear.

Good looks like

It finds what you would have found and points to where. Spot check two or three items.

Move up

When your spot checks keep coming back clean.

Step 3 of 6

Give it a folder

Try

Give it three framing bids for one job and ask for a side by side spreadsheet with the questions to ask each bidder. Folder work is best in the desktop app (more below).

Good looks like

It shows where the bids are not apples to apples instead of just ranking by price. You check the totals.

Move up

When it reads across many files and stays careful with details.

Step 4 of 6

Set up a standing project

Try

Create a project for one job with the plans, specifications and your scope template, then ask for the tile sub's scope of work in your format.

Good looks like

It uses your format without reminders and flags what the plans don't specify instead of guessing.

Move up

When you stop re-explaining your company every conversation.

Step 5 of 6

Let it draft something you would send

Try

Give it a change order, the cost breakdown and the reason, and ask for a short letter to the client.

Good looks like

It reads like you and every number matches the source. You check it and send it yourself.

Move up

When your edits shrink to a few words.

Step 6 of 6

Let it act through a connection

Try

Connect your email and ask what was agreed with the electrician about the panel upgrade.

Good looks like

It finds the right thread, does only what you asked and tells you what it did.

Stop climbing

Whenever you want.

Projects and instructions

A project is a workspace for one area of your business. For a construction company, each job is its own project with its own instructions and its own files. On Team that project is shared (with view or edit access), so the whole office and Claude stay on the same page. Estimating and warranty make good projects for the recurring work.

Each project has standing instructions. Write them like a brief for a new hire:

Example instructionsWrite in our company voice: direct, plain, no jargon. Summarize meetings under three headings: decisions, next steps, open questions. Never invent numbers, dates or names. If something is not in the attached files, say so.

If you correct the same thing twice, it belongs in the instructions.

Files, folders and the desktop app

The desktop app is where Claude can work with folders on your computer: a set of plans, a month of subcontractor invoices, the closeout documents for a finished job.

It can also hand back real files: Word documents, Excel spreadsheets and slide decks you can open and edit.

A few habits to start with:

  • Work on a copy of a folder until you know how it behaves.
  • Check spreadsheet totals against your accounting system.
  • Read, edit and iterate every time, almost without exception.
Caution

Plans and drawings. Claude reads everything written on a set of plans well: notes, schedules, specifications and dimensions as text. What it is not good at is the drawing itself, meaning where lines sit, what is next to what and how spaces relate. Treat anything about layout or location as unverified and check it against the sheet yourself.

TipAsk Claude to index your plans when a job starts. An indexed set is cheaper and easier to search and refer back to over the life of the job.

Connections to the tools you already use

Start with your email

If you connect one thing, connect your email. For a contractor or small business owner, this is the single biggest jump in usefulness. With email connected you can ask:

  • What did we agree with the plumber about the rough-in date?
  • Catch me up on this thread with the architect before tomorrow's meeting.
  • Find the email with the revised window quote attached.
  • Draft a reply to the homeowner about the tile delay.

Teach it your voice right away. Once your email is connected, ask Claude to read your sent mail and learn how you write. That is the fastest, cleanest way for it to pick up your voice, and the moment to do it is right when you connect. Save what it writes about your style into your project instructions so it sticks.

Gmail is read and search only, so Claude cannot send. It drafts and you send. Treat Outlook the same way.

What else you can connect

Connections (Anthropic calls them connectors) fall into three groups.

  • Your email and calendar. Gmail and Google Calendar, or Microsoft 365 for Outlook mail and calendar. Microsoft 365 needs a work account and a one-time approval from whoever administers your company's Microsoft account. Personal outlook.com addresses do not work.
  • Your files. Google Drive, Dropbox and Box, plus OneDrive and SharePoint through Microsoft 365.
  • Your office tools. QuickBooks (more below), Slack, Asana, Notion and HubSpot.

Construction software

Where the common builder tools stand as of October 2026:

  • JobTread has an official JobTread AI Connector, built by JobTread. You add it by pasting the address JobTread gives you into Claude's connector settings, and JobTread's instructions make the setup easy.
  • SketchUp has an official connector that works through claude.ai. You need a Trimble ID.
  • QuickBooks connects through a verified connector built by Pilot that reads and writes QuickBooks Online.
  • Buildertrend, Procore, CompanyCam and Houzz Pro have no official connections, only third-party ones.
  • Bluebeam: reports of something in beta, not yet confirmed.

Verisk XactRestore (restoration estimating) and Autodesk Fusion also have official connectors.

The rule: check your software provider's help pages for "Claude" or "MCP" (the technical name for these connections). More vendors are adding them every month, and when one exists the setup is a paste-in, not a technical project. Where there is none, exports, PDFs and email still get you most of the way.

Skills

A skill is a set of reusable instructions saved in a folder. Claude picks it up on its own when a task matches. Skills are on the paid plans, and on Team they can be shared with the whole company.

My method for making one:

  1. Do the task once with Claude, really well, iterating until the output is exactly right.
  2. Ask Claude to turn that conversation into a skill.
  3. Use it, and fix the skill whenever you catch yourself correcting the same thing.

A few we use in my office, all written this way: a bid comparison that lines up several proposals against one scope and lists the gaps, a trade scope of work generator that drafts a sub's scope from the plans and our template, a plan set comparison that reads two revisions and lists what changed, an invoice processor that checks a vendor invoice against the order before it is filed, delivery labels for materials headed to a job and a procedure writer that turns a recorded explanation into a written SOP.

The first one most offices would want is a change order memo in your own format: the header, the sections in order (what changed, why, cost, schedule effect, what you need from the client) and the rules (exact numbers, never rounded, plain and calm). After that, anyone in the office can say "write up change order 14 for the client" and get your format.

Plans and privacy

Prices as of October 2026. Check claude.com/pricing before you decide.

  • Pro: $20 a month ($17 a month billed annually). This is where I would start. You are doing business work with business data, so the paid plan is the starting line, not an upgrade. It includes Claude Code.
  • Team: $25 a seat a month ($20 billed annually), minimum two seats. Move here when more than one person uses it. Team is where the office shares projects and skills, and where your data is not used for training by default.
  • Max: from $100 a month. For heavy work with plans and estimating, or for the person on your team who will build the tools and apps.
  • Free: $0. Not for business work.

On Free, Pro and Max, your conversations can be used to train Anthropic's models unless you turn that off. On Pro, turn off "Help improve Claude" in Settings, Privacy the day you sign up. On Team and Enterprise, conversations are not used for training by default.

Before you build anything, take a walk

When you are ready for real system work (skills, custom tools), don't start at the keyboard. Start with a walk.

Go on a hike or somewhere quiet, open Claude on your phone and turn on voice mode. Voice mode is on every plan and works best on the phone. Then talk through every business problem, inefficiency, worry and wish you can think of. Don't organize it. Just get it out.

When you run dry, ask Claude which of those an AI is best suited to help with. You come away with a list. Then you start attacking the list.

That is how I started.

Claude Code: when you want to build something

Claude Code is a way to write software and automate things by describing them in English. It has more access to your computer than regular Claude: it can create and change files in a folder, run programs and connect to other systems. That is what lets it build real tools, not just documents.

It lives in the Code tab of the desktop app. You don't need a terminal, and it requires a paid plan.

The biggest barrier to programming was always the language, not the ideas. Now you describe what you want in English and the code comes out the other side. It takes some learning and some time to get comfortable, but you do not need to be technical. It is the same habit as everything else here: describe, look, correct, go again.

I have built a number of custom workflows and programs this way. One of them is Walkthrough on the App Store, an app my crew uses to turn a site walk with photos and talking into a report.

A plain-English glossary

TermWhat it means
TerminalA text window where you type commands to your computer.
Directory or folderThe same thing. Programmers say directory.
Repository (repo)The folder that holds a project's code and its history.
GitThe tool that keeps that history so you can see changes and undo them.
CommitA saved snapshot of the project with a note about what changed.
BranchA separate copy for trying something without touching the working version.
CodeThe written instructions a computer follows.
RunStart a program and let it do its work.
DeployPut your program where other people can use it.
APIA doorway one program offers so other programs can talk to it.
Environment variableA setting kept outside the code, often a password or key.
Local vs cloudLocal means on your own computer. In Claude Code, cloud means Anthropic's computers running the work for you, not cloud storage like Dropbox.
BugSomething in the program that doesn't work the way you meant.
PromptWhat you type to the AI.
ModelThe AI itself, the engine behind Claude.
TokenA small piece of text. Usage and limits are counted in tokens.
ContextEverything the AI can see at once: your messages, files and instructions.
